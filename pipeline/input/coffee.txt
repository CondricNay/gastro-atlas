Coffee is a beverage made from the roasted seeds of plants in the Coffea genus. The coffee plant is an evergreen shrub that grows best in tropical climates. Its seeds contain caffeine, a naturally occurring compound that acts as a stimulant. Coffee beans are commonly roasted at high temperatures, which changes their color, aroma, and flavor.

The earliest reliable accounts of coffee drinking come from Yemen in the 15th century. Sufi monks in Yemen are reported to have used coffee to help them stay awake during nighttime prayers. Coffee cultivation and preparation developed in Yemen, and the drink became associated with religious communities in the region.

By the 16th century, coffee had spread from Yemen to other parts of the Arabian Peninsula, including Mecca and Medina. Coffeehouses began appearing in cities across the region. These establishments became places where people could drink coffee, talk, listen to music, and exchange news.

Coffee reached the Ottoman Empire during the 16th century. Coffeehouses became established in Istanbul, and the drink became increasingly popular among urban residents. Ottoman coffeehouses served as social gathering places and were sometimes associated with political discussion and public debate.

During the 17th century, coffee was introduced to Europe. Venetian merchants traded coffee with the Ottoman Empire, and coffee began to appear in Venice. The first coffeehouses were established in England in the middle of the 17th century. Oxford opened a coffeehouse in 1654, while the first coffeehouse in London is traditionally associated with Pasqua Rosée and appeared around 1652.

Coffeehouses became popular in London during the late 17th century. They attracted merchants, writers, scientists, and other members of the public. Some coffeehouses became associated with particular professions. Lloyd's Coffee House, for example, became a meeting place for merchants and people involved in maritime insurance during the late 17th century.

Coffee cultivation expanded beyond Arabia during the 17th and 18th centuries. The Dutch established coffee cultivation in their colonies, including Java in the Dutch East Indies. Coffee grown in Java became an important commercial product and contributed to the expansion of coffee production outside the Arabian Peninsula.

In the 18th century, European colonial powers established coffee plantations in tropical regions of the Americas and the Caribbean. Coffee production expanded in places such as the French Caribbean, Brazil, and other parts of Latin America. Brazil eventually became one of the world's major coffee-producing regions.

Coffee became an important commodity in international trade during the 18th and 19th centuries. Growing demand encouraged the expansion of plantations and international trading networks. The history of coffee production was also connected to colonial labor systems and, in several regions, enslaved labor.

Coffee contains several chemical compounds, including caffeine, chlorogenic acids, and other substances produced or altered during roasting. The exact chemical composition varies depending on the coffee species, growing conditions, processing method, and roasting process.

Brazil is now the world's largest producer of coffee, and coffee is consumed in many countries. Modern coffee production includes large commercial plantations as well as small farms. Espresso, filter coffee, and instant coffee are among the many forms in which coffee is consumed today.
